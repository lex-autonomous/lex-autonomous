Phase 1 — Topic Research Protocol
Run this before writing any script or building any calendar slot.
Step 1: Topic Validation Check
Before committing to a topic, run these four checks:

 Utility check — Can this be reduced to a skill or tool someone uses this week? If not, find the angle that makes it practical.
 Audience check — Does this speak to someone who feels behind on AI, not someone already deep in it?
 Pillar check — Which pillar does this map to? If it doesn't map cleanly, it's probably too vague.
 Novelty trap check — Is this topic being covered because it's trending, or because it's genuinely useful right now? Trending alone is not enough.

Step 2: Competitive Angle Scan
Before writing, answer:

What's the obvious version of this video? (The one every other channel will make)
What does Kaiya say that nobody else is saying about this?
Where is the "here's what most people miss" moment in this topic?

The Kaiya angle is never the obvious one. It's always one layer deeper.

Phase 2 — Script Writing
Full-Length Script Structure
Every main video follows this structure. The order is not flexible.
1. Hook (0:00–0:30)
The most important 30 seconds of the video. Does one job: make the viewer
decide to stay. Kaiya's hooks are not clickbait — they're honest previews
of the actual payoff.
Hook formula options:

The Honest Promise: "In the next 12 minutes, I'm going to show you exactly how to [specific outcome]. Not the overview version. The actual thing."
The Contrarian Open: "Everyone is talking about [X]. Here's what nobody's saying about it."
The Relatable Problem: "If you've ever [specific frustrating situation], this video is going to fix that."
The Result First: Start with the outcome, then explain how you get there.

What a Kaiya hook never does:

"In today's video, we're going to be talking about..."
Fake urgency ("before it's too late")
Vague promise ("this will change how you think about AI")
Asking for likes/subscribes before delivering value

2. Credibility Moment (0:30–1:00)
One or two sentences. Not a full bio — a specific reason why she's the
right person to explain this thing right now. Tied to the topic directly.
Example: "I spent two years watching enterprise teams fail at AI adoption
before they ever touched the tools. The problem was never the technology."
3. Setup / Context (1:00–2:30)
Frame the problem or question the video answers. This is where Kaiya earns
the audience's trust by showing she understands their actual situation.
Rules:

Stay with the audience's perspective, not the expert's perspective
Name the specific frustration or gap they're feeling
Do not start explaining the solution yet — build the need first

4. Core Content (2:30–end minus 2 min)
The actual teaching. Organized in clear, logical sections with natural
transitions. Each section ends before moving to the next — no meandering.
Section structure inside core content:

State what this section covers (one sentence)
Explain it in plain English
Show or demonstrate where possible (prompt on screen, tool demo, example)
Land the "part most people skip" moment where relevant
Short transition to next section

Kaiya's teaching style inside the core:

Never more than 2–3 sentences before a concrete example
If it gets abstract, anchor it with "Okay real talk — in practice this means..."
Dry observations welcome — the unexpectedness is part of the value
"Stay with me for a second" signals a connection between two non-obvious things

5. The Real Talk Moment (somewhere in core content)
Every video has at least one "Okay, real talk" moment where Kaiya says the
honest version everyone else is too polished to say. This is often the most
shared clip. Build it deliberately — don't leave it to chance.
6. CTA (end minus 1:30)
Low-friction, confidence without hype. Kaiya does not beg for engagement.
CTA formula: Specific ask + reason it benefits them + "Try it once. You'll see."
Example: "If you want to test this yourself, the prompt I used is in the
description. Copy it, try it on your own project, see what happens.
Try it once. You'll see."
7. Sign-Off

"That's it. Real skill, real life. Try it this week — and if something
breaks, come back and tell me. I genuinely want to know. I'll see you
in the next one."

Phase 3 — Titles and Thumbnails
Title Rules
YouTube titles are not headlines. They serve the algorithm and the
human simultaneously. Kaiya's titles do both without becoming clickbait.
Title formula options:

The Skill Title: "How to [specific thing] with AI (the part nobody shows you)"
The Problem Title: "Why [common approach] doesn't work — and what actually does"
The Honest Breakdown: "I tested [tool/method] for [time period]. Here's what happened."
The Reframe: "[Common belief about AI]. Here's what's actually true."
The Specific Result: "[Specific outcome] in [time] using AI — here's the exact process"

Title rules:

Under 60 characters where possible (full visibility on mobile)
No ALL CAPS unless it's a single word for emphasis
No "YOU WON'T BELIEVE" or fake shock language
Specificity beats vagueness every time — "3 prompts" beats "some prompts"
The parenthetical is Kaiya's secret weapon — it's where she adds the honest angle

Deliver 3 title options for every video. Label each with the formula used.
Thumbnail Direction (Notes Only)
Kaiya's thumbnails follow these rules — include as a note in the script
delivery, not as a design task:

Clean background, high contrast
Kaiya's expression: curious or dry — never shocked/mouth-open
Text overlay: 3–5 words max, completing or contrasting the title
No red arrows or zoom circles unless genuinely pointing to something
Warm, natural lighting — not studio-slick


Phase 4 — Short-Form Adaptation
Every main video should yield 2–3 Shorts. Identify extraction moments
during scripting, not after.
What Makes a Good Short Extraction
Best Short moments from a Kaiya video:

The "Okay, real talk" moment — standalone, high shareability
A single tip or technique demonstrated completely in under 60 seconds
The "here's what nobody's saying" reframe — often the most surprising moment
A live prompt demonstration with visible output
A clean "try this one thing" micro-lesson

Short Script Structure
SHORT TITLE: [Different from the main title — optimized for short-form discovery]
SOURCE: [Main video title + timestamp range]
DURATION: [Target seconds]

HOOK (0–3 seconds): [Pattern interrupt — one line, no intro]
CONTENT (3–50 seconds): [The one thing — demonstrated or explained completely]
CLOSE (50–60 seconds): [Sign-off: "Real skill. Try it."]
Short-Form Rules

Hook in the first 3 seconds or it's already lost
No "in today's video" — Shorts have no patience for preamble
One point only — never try to compress a full video
Kaiya's dry humor lands especially well in Shorts — lean into it
Caption everything — most Shorts are watched on mute

Phase 6 — Trend Decode Protocol
When a major AI development drops and Kaiya needs to respond quickly:

Read before writing — pull 3–5 sources on the development before forming the take
Find the "what this actually means" angle — not a recap, but the practical implication for her audience
Identify the overhyped claim — there's always one; naming it earns trust
Structure: Hook → What happened (plain English, 90 seconds max) → What this actually means for you → What to do with this information → Sign-off
Target length: 3–5 minutes — fast, sharp, no padding

Trend Decode scripts follow the same voice audit as all other content.
Speed of publication does not lower the voice standard.
